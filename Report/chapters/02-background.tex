\subsection{System Architecture}
\subsubsection{Monolithic Architecture}

Monolithic architecture refers to a traditional software design pattern in which all components of an application --- including user interface, business logic, and data access layers --- are packaged and deployed as a single (Figure~\ref{fig:monolithic-architecture}), unified unit. In this model, the application runs as one process, often supported by a single codebase and a centralized database. \cite{thalor2024monolithic} describe monolithic systems as tightly coupled, meaning that changes in one part of the application often necessitate redeployment of the entire system.

The primary advantage of monolithic architecture lies in its simplicity. It is straightforward to develop, test, and deploy, making it particularly suitable for small teams and minimum viable products (MVPs). Since all components share the same memory space and runtime environment, monolithic applications also benefit from reduced communication overhead compared to distributed systems. This architecture enables rapid iteration during early development phases, where time-to-market and cost efficiency are prioritized.

\subsubsection{Domain Driven Design (DDD)}

Domain-Driven Design (DDD) is a software engineering methodology that integrates software architecture with the business domain to address the complexity of large-scale systems. Its primary objective is to ensure that software models accurately represent business processes, thereby shifting development from a technology-oriented to a domain-oriented perspective. \cite{ozkan2025ddd}

Two foundational concepts define DDD: Ubiquitous Language and Bounded Contexts. Ubiquitous Language is a collaboratively developed vocabulary shared between developers and domain experts. By embedding this language directly into communication and source code, DDD eliminates ambiguity and strengthens the alignment between the software model and the business domain. Bounded Contexts, in contrast, establish explicit boundaries around sub-domains, each maintaining its own model and terminology. This separation reduces complexity, prevents inconsistencies, and allows development teams to focus on smaller, coherent parts of the system.

Beyond conceptual clarity, DDD offers practical architectural benefits. The separation provided by Bounded Contexts naturally aligns with modern distributed paradigms such as microservices, where autonomous and loosely coupled services are essential. This approach enables systems to evolve alongside changing business requirements while maintaining technical robustness and strategic alignment with organizational goals. \cite{ozkan2025ddd}
